\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Multi-step LLM workflows---where each inference step feeds the next---have emerged as a
standard architecture for deploying language models in real applications. Frameworks such
as LangChain \cite{chase2022}, LangGraph \cite{langgraph2024}, AutoGen \cite{wu2023},
and PromptFlow \cite{microsoft2023} have made such workflows easy to build. Production
systems now routinely orchestrate dozens of LLM calls across stages for coding
assistance, document analysis, retrieval and verification, and large-scale matching
tasks.

Despite their practical success, these workflows remain difficult to debug, resume, and
improve systematically. A central reason is that the state of computation is often only
partially explicit. Dependencies are carried forward through conversation histories,
mutable runtime objects, prompt templates, and framework-specific control logic.
Consequently, an intermediate artifact at step $k$ may not be a complete representation
of the computation at that point: it may rely on inherited context from earlier steps
that is not itself captured as part of the checkpoint. When this happens, failure
recovery becomes brittle, replay is hard to trust, and debugging requires reconstructing
a large fraction of the prior execution.

Tooling can alleviate parts of this problem through persistence, tracing, and state
save/load. But the underlying limitation remains: current orchestration stacks generally
lack a shared representation of reasoning itself. The plan, its intermediate semantics,
and the conditions for valid continuation are scattered across code, prompts, and runtime
conventions rather than expressed in a single interpretable form.

\textbf{What is missing is a reasoning medium.} AI systems need a language in which
multi-step reasoning procedures can be explicitly represented, inspected, verified,
executed, and refined over time. This medium should play, for reasoning workflows, a
role analogous to SQL for data access or formal notation for mathematics. Without it,
reasoning remains opaque to non-developers, difficult to audit, hard to reproduce, and
resistant to cumulative improvement. A viable reasoning medium must therefore be
simultaneously human-readable, machine-executable, and structurally enforceable.

\textbf{NormCode is that medium.} NormCode \cite{anon2025} is a semi-formal planning
language for LLM workflows with six properties that together constitute a working
reasoning medium: \textit{Interpretable} (readable by multiple roles without programming
expertise), \textit{Enforceable} (compiler-verified structure that the orchestrator
executes exactly as reviewed), \textit{Composable} (three symbols and one scope rule,
unlimited depth), \textit{Locally Addressable} (every step has a precise flow index;
every step is epistemically self-contained by the scope rule), \textit{Generalizable}
(domain-agnostic primitives for any workflow), and \textit{Portable} (plain text, any
compliant orchestrator).

The scope rule is the structural mechanism: each step's inputs are declared explicitly
in an indented block, and the compiler verifies that no step accesses data outside its
block. The orchestrator enforces this at runtime. A checkpoint captured under this
guarantee is a \textbf{self-contained case}: its tensor contains exactly the data
downstream steps will use, with no implicit context dependency.

\textbf{When a reasoning medium has these six properties, Case-Based Reasoning is what
you get --- at two levels simultaneously.} This is the central claim of this paper.
The two levels emerge from the medium's structure, not from additional engineering:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Level~1 (Concrete Case Management):} Every completed execution
    checkpoint is a \textit{suspended NormCode runtime} --- the full Blackboard (node
    status map) and Concept Repository (all tensors for all completed nodes) at a
    specific flow index. The four CBR operations map directly onto Canvas interactions:
    Retrieve (Tensor Inspector), Reuse (Fork), Revise (Value Override with automatic
    stale-boundary computation), Retain (automatic checkpointing at every completed
    node).

  \item \textbf{Level~2 (Abstract Case Learning):} Every compiled NormCode plan is a
    \textit{runtime definition} --- an abstract case specifying what all executions of
    that task type are possible. Four production-ready plans serve as an initial case
    library of abstract reasoning patterns. The compilation pipeline
    (Derivation $\rightarrow$ Formalization $\rightarrow$ Post-Formalization $\rightarrow$
    Activation) is the distillation process; it is itself a NormCode plan --- enabling
    recursive case-based learning.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{NormCode Canvas (v1.1.3)} is the deployed system that exposes both levels
through an interface for tools, agents, and plan execution. Three practical properties
follow from the reasoning medium's design: \textbf{(C1)~constant-effort failure
localization} --- at any breakpoint, the Tensor Inspector reveals the explicit inputs to
step $k$ directly, because each step's scope is declared and enforced rather than
reconstructed from history; \textbf{(C2)~pre-execution structural review} --- the
compiler's Formalization phase produces a \texttt{.ncn} narrative before any LLM call,
allowing a domain expert to inspect and reject the plan if its structure does not match
the intended reasoning (unlike RLHF \cite{ouyang2022} or Constitutional AI \cite{bai2022}, 
this review operates on the explicit plan before any computation begins);
and \textbf{(C3)~scope-bounded selective re-execution} --- when a case is revised at
flow index $f$, only transitively downstream nodes re-execute; unaffected upstream
results are reused from cache.

\textbf{Relation to the companion language paper \cite{anon2025}.} The companion paper
is a language specification: it defines NormCode's grammar, symbol set, type system,
and scope rule, and gives a formal account of the execution architecture. That paper
answers \textit{what NormCode is}. This paper answers \textit{what NormCode makes
possible}. In demonstration of its applicability, we present four production-ready
workflows ranging from PPT generation and code assistance to self-hosted NormCode
compilation.
